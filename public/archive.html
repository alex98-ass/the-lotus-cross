Six texts we actually read — not the ten thousand we quote

Every tradition here has a library. We are not going to pretend to have read it. These are the six works that shaped this community, in free public-domain translations, with an honest introduction to each.

The texts

Free, legal, public domain
Buddhist

The Dhammapada

Attributed to the Buddha · trans. Max Müller, 1881

Four hundred and twenty-three verses, and no story to hide behind. It is the closest thing we have to the Buddha's own voice, and it is almost entirely about one thing: the mind precedes everything you do. Read it slowly, one verse at a time. It does not reward speed, and it does not comfort.

Hindu

The Song Celestial — Bhagavad Gita

from the Mahabharata · trans. Edwin Arnold, 1885

A conversation on a battlefield between a man who wants to stop and God in the form of a charioteer. It is the text that gave us the three tracks — love, awareness, right action — and the most honest answer anyone has written to the question "why should I keep doing my duty when it costs me everything?"

Read it free →
Christian

The Imitation of Christ

Thomas à Kempis, 15th century

The most-read Christian book after the Bible, and the least sentimental. It says, plainly, that most of your suffering is your own opinion of yourself, and that the cure is not intensity but ordinary fidelity. If it feels cold on the first page, keep going — it is honest, not cold.

Read it free →
Christian mystical

The Cloud of Unknowing

Anonymous English, 14th century

Written by a priest for a young man who wanted to pray and could not. Its argument is startling and very close to Zen: God cannot be reached by thinking, so the work is to place a cloud of forgetting between you and everything you know, and love into the dark. This is the bridge between the two halves of this community.

Read it free →
Taoist

Tao Te Ching

Laozi · trans. James Legge, 1891

Eighty-one short chapters that refuse to be a doctrine. We include it because the practice of doing less, and doing it properly, is the hardest part of any spiritual life — and because it keeps us honest about how much of our religion is performance.

Read it free →
Hindu

The Upanishads — the Isa and Katha

trans. Max Müller, 1879–1884

Not a book to finish but a few pages to sit with. The Isa says the whole world is covered by the Lord, and therefore what you are grasping at is already yours. The Katha is a young man asking death itself what happens after. Start with those two and leave the rest for later.

Read it free →

The twelve weeks

One portion a week

This is the order we read them in, and the question we ask each week. It is not a course and there is no certificate — it is simply a way to keep reading when the enthusiasm of the first week has gone.

Week 1
Dhammapada 1–2
Which verse describes a mind I recognise as mine?
Week 2
Imitation, Book I 1–3
Where am I performing rather than practising?
Week 3
Gita 1–2
What is my duty when I would rather withdraw?
Week 4
Cloud of Unknowing 1–3
What do I keep thinking about instead of praying?
Week 5
Tao 1–10
What would doing less look like this week?
Week 6
Dhammapada 3–4
What am I clinging to that has already gone?
Week 7
Imitation, Book II
What comfort am I unwilling to give up?
Week 8
Gita 3–4
Where does my action come from — fear or offering?
Week 9
Isa Upanishad
What would change if everything were already sacred?
Week 10
Tao 11–20
Which emptiness am I afraid of?
Week 11
Katha Upanishad
What would I do differently if this year were the last?
Week 12
Gita 12, then return
What has changed, and what was I only pretending to change?
Why public-domain translations. Copyright protects the translator, not the saint. Every text above is old enough to be free of rights in this form, which means we can point you to the full thing at no cost and without asking permission. Where a modern translation is better — Easwaran's Gita, for instance — we say so, but we link to the one you can read today for nothing.

Nothing here is scripture in the sense of "inerrant". It is what we read. If a text stops being useful to you, put it down; that is not a failure of faith.
